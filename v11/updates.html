I have always enjoyed economics and finance, but actually doing research in them has been quite different from studying them in a course or preparing for a competition. There isn't always a correct answer waiting at the end, and sometimes the result you get creates more questions than the one you started with. I think that is also what has made it interesting for me. A lot of this work has happened through YRI , where I have had the chance to work on the paper, present it, get feedback and learn from people who have much more experience with research than I do. Students are building resumes faster than they are building skills.

“Students are building resumes faster than they are building skills.” Been thinking about this a lot recently. Internships, competitions, certifications, fellowships. Over time, many of these have stopped being just learning experiences and also become signals. Not “What did you learn?” but “What have you collected?” Tried breaking this down using 6 lenses for #ThinkIn6 by Restless Dreamers . Economics: Internships now carry signalling value beyond skill development. Even unpaid opportunities receive massive demand because students see them as leverage for placements, college admissions, networking, and future credibility. Psychology: A lot of students apply not purely out of interest, but out of fear of falling behind. Constant exposure to acceptance posts and achievement announcements creates pressure to keep accumulating experiences, even without enough depth in any one of them. Marketing: Many student programs are marketed around exclusivity, prestige, and acceptance rates rather than actual learning outcomes. Sometimes the perception of selectivity becomes more valuable than the experience itself. Statistics: The scale of this shift becomes visible in the data itself. According to the Unstop Talent Report 2026, 92% of B-School students, 95% of engineering students, and 93% of UG students are willing to accept a lower CTC for learning and growth opportunities. Do let me know your views about the same if you think I missed anything or anything else should be added Over the past few months, direct strikes, retaliation, and continued tension have created sustained instability. And that instability is now shaping decisions far beyond the region. Tried breaking this down using 6 lenses. Economics: Disruptions and rising risk around the Strait of Hormuz have already increased shipping costs, insurance premiums, and oil price volatility. The added risk itself acts like a cost on global trade. Moreover, due to psychological factors the international revenue of countries involved is being hampered a lot. Psychology: Initial reactions were driven by real events like missile strikes. But what follows is driven by uncertainty. People adjust behaviour not just to what happened, but to what might happen next. That is why caution persists even after the event. Due to this travel plans have changed for a lot of people and majorly education plans for international students wishing to pursue higher education in middle east. Marketing: Stability itself becomes a product during conflict. Countries and regions now have to actively signal safety, continuity, and reliability to investors, students, and businesses.
