DPS CHANDIGARH · 2025–26

Deepshikha — the student company

A school enterprise with shareholders, stalls, team decisions and financial literacy.

The context

We ran a school company with real shareholders, products, events and money to account for.

My role

I served as Managing Director from April 2025 to March 2026, coordinating a roughly 50-member team and five stalls.

What we did

I led sales and operations planning, team coordination and shareholder communication. I also co-convened Financial Literacy Week, with 45 volunteers across 49 rooms and activities involving more than 1,000 students.

The result

Our company reported 540% profit. Financial Literacy Week reached more than 1,000 students, and the programme combined sales experience with practical financial education.

Team & collaborators

Student company team of approximately 50; supported by teachers and student contributors

In my words

The Instagram photographs show what the company looked like day to day: planning meetings, stalls, sales and events. I learned as much from organising the people as from the numbers.

Shareholders, not just spectators

More than 300 shareholders put ₹10 per share into the student company. Beyond the share register was a core operating team and over 50 students working across stalls, events and day-to-day tasks. I had to balance enthusiasm with budgets, division of responsibilities and a simple question: would people actually buy what we made?

Managing Director sounded like a big title, but much of my day was chasing decisions, helping people coordinate and making sure a project could survive the moment we stopped discussing it.

Financial Literacy Week · 11–14 August 2025

We reached over 1,000 students across nearly 50 classrooms. The classroom quiz rounds generated approximately 1,050–1,100 response sheets, and 32 finalists moved forward. Making those numbers possible required scheduling rooms, checking papers, supervising junior-class activities, arranging materials, coordinating the auditorium and addressing problems when plans changed.

The event involved financial learning through activities rather than treating finance as a list of intimidating terms. The team reported approximately ₹60,000 in profit and a dividend of 2.5 times the original share value. I remember the operational questions as clearly as the outcome: which volunteer would cover a room, how we could avoid confusion and who could solve a problem immediately.

What I actually learned

Financial statements are one view of a company; the energy and friction of a large team are another. At Deepshikha, the hardest decisions rarely came with a formula. They involved asking someone for help, changing a plan without discouraging the people who had worked on it, and keeping the business credible to its shareholders.
