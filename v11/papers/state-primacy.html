HISTORICAL POLICY RESEARCH

Seventy years is not one reform.

The long view

Our manuscript traces changes from state-led industrialisation and the licensing system through the 1991 liberalisation period toward recent entrepreneurship institutions and startup-support mechanisms. We wanted to explain how state priorities, laws and opportunities for private enterprise changed together—not merely tell a simple story of “before” and “after.”

What we examined

The approach centred on legal and policy documents, historical periods and institutional shifts rather than interviewing startup founders or estimating a single event's causal effect. This makes chronology, selection of documents and clarity in describing mechanisms especially important.

The reviewers' strongest point

Feedback called for a reproducible coding rubric, additional India-specific sources and a clearer timeline. A change introduced in 2025 cannot explain a 2016–2024 outcome without an explicit argument and mechanism. This led us to revise how we connected reforms with entrepreneurship indicators.

The Finelytics collaboration

Team Finelytics included Karan Aggarwal, Basma Ehab Basuoiny and Namratha Chowdary Mogalapu. The paper emerged from NSRI's summer research hackathon and continued into a revised manuscript. Our responsibilities were collaborative; this is not a sole-author historical study.

What we can conclude

The document analysis can clarify changes in institutional priorities and legal frameworks. It is not, without additional data and identification, proof that a single act caused any particular increase in business creation. That distinction is central to presenting the work responsibly.

Why the question matters

It is easy to celebrate an entrepreneurship ecosystem without asking which rules make it possible for a small founder to participate. Studying the long-run institutional background reminded me that enterprise policy is shaped as much by implementation and continuity as by the headline announcement.
