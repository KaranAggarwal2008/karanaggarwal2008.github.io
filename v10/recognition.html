John Locke Institute Global Essay Prize / 2026

Eight commendations. Eight different ways of asking why.

The reward was recognition, but the real project was learning to think across disciplines without flattening them into one answer.

In 2026, eight of my essays received commendations in the John Locke Institute Global Essay Prize: International Relations, Public Policy, Theology, Philosophy, Psychology, Economics, Law, and Science & Technology. I attended the London ceremony. Each category asked for its own kind of reasoning, and I resisted treating all eight as versions of the same argument.

01 / International Relations

Power, interests and the gap between principle and practice.

International relations interests me because governments speak in the language of principle while making choices under very unequal constraints. I wanted to examine how ideals, incentives and institutions collide—and why seeing another state's incentives isn't the same as endorsing its actions.

02 / Public Policy

What looks fair on paper may fail in practice.

Policy arguments must address implementation, political incentives and the people affected. I was interested in how an apparently straightforward reform changes once one asks who bears its costs, how administrators respond and what evidence could tell us it works.

03 / Theology

Belief deserves more than a caricature.

Exploring theology pushed me beyond treating faith only as a sociological fact or only as a private assertion. Arguments about belief raise questions of interpretation, justification and what people take to be authoritative.

04 / Philosophy

Interrogate the premise before the conclusion.

I was drawn to philosophy's habit of asking whether we have framed the problem properly. Sometimes a disagreement persists because people use the same words for different ideas or assume different moral starting points.

05 / Psychology

We aren't always the reliable narrators of our own decisions.

Psychology connects strongly with my interest in incentives. It's easy to assume people respond predictably to a price or a rule; harder to explain attention, bias, identity and the limits of introspection.

06 / Economics

Incentives matter, but context matters too.

I enjoy economic reasoning because it makes trade-offs more visible. At the same time, models become most interesting when we ask where their assumptions stop matching how institutions, families or businesses actually behave.

07 / Law

The difference between a right and its exercise.

Legal questions can sound settled when a principle is stated. The more difficult part is interpretation, enforcement, procedure and unequal access to the institutions meant to make rights meaningful.

08 / Science & Technology

New capability doesn't settle who should control it.

Technology debates often rush from “can we” to “should we.” I was interested in the institutional choices around technical change: who gains access, who sets standards, and how public responsibilities adjust.

These notes describe the questions that attract me in each field, rather than presenting reconstructed quotations or invented summaries of my original essay texts.

Why I kept writing across categories

One of the most useful parts of the competition was being forced to leave my preferred vocabulary behind. Philosophy doesn't let you hide behind an economics diagram; law asks for institutional precision; psychology requires attention to how people actually behave. I came away with more questions than answers, which seems like a good outcome for an essay competition.
