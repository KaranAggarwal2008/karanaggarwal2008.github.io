Behavioural economics / online marketplaces / collaborative research

Do low opening bids make buyers bid more?

It was an appealing intuition. The actual data gave us reasons to be much more careful.

Strategic Under-Pricing in Online Auctions: Testing the Moderating Roles of Seller Reputation and Bidder Competition on eBay.

The question

Online auctions create an unusual trade-off. A seller who starts with a low price might attract more attention and competitive bidding—but might also signal weak value or leave money on the table. We wanted to know whether that intuitive “low starting price” strategy really translated into higher final prices, and whether seller reputation or competition changed the relationship.

Two complementary datasets

We analysed 628 historical completed auction records from a publicly available dataset, covering 136 Cartier items, 343 Palm Pilots and 149 Xbox listings. To study reputation in more current conditions, we independently gathered 200 completed eBay auctions, of which 187 were usable for that analysis. The historical and newly gathered data have different strengths, so we did not treat them as automatically interchangeable.

How we analysed it

The study used ordinary least squares regressions with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, transformations including logarithmic specifications, and tests of interactions involving starting bid, seller reputation and bidder competition. Each choice was about separating an intuitive narrative from what the observed outcomes could support.

What we found

Contrary to the strongest version of the low-starting-price story, opening bid was positively associated with final price in our analyses. We did not establish that artificially low opening prices increased the eventual sale price. Nor did the proposed moderators provide decisive statistical support: the seller-reputation interaction was reported at approximately β = 247.69, p = .848, and bidder-competition interaction at β = 12.56, p = .149. Those are not statistically significant at conventional levels.

Why the caution matters

The data are observational. Auction categories, listing characteristics, seller behaviour and omitted differences can influence both starting and closing prices. The paper is useful for challenging a simplistic prescriptive claim, not for asserting a universal causal law for sellers. We also explored robustness checks; these do not turn an observational design into an experiment.

Finance / geopolitical risk / YRI mentored research

How does a security shock reach the stock market?

My study on the 2016 Uri attack examines how Indian equity markets and selected sectors responded to a major geopolitical event, and how to distinguish an immediate shock from a more persistent change.

Why this question

Markets are often described as efficient processors of information, but news about security and geopolitics arrives in imperfect ways. Investors may respond not only to an event, but to uncertainty about retaliation, escalation and its economic consequences. The Uri attack on 18 September 2016 and subsequent developments offered a case for examining those patterns.

Design and data

The paper used financial-market observations sourced through registered market-data services and publicly accessible financial information, including BSE-related data retrieved via Yahoo Finance and Motilal APIs. It does not involve interviews or personal human-participant data. We studied windows around 18 September and the 29 September surgical-strikes announcement, including pre-event, event and post-event comparisons, alternative event dates and difference-in-differences specifications.

Sectoral and firm-level observations

Part of the work explored how firms such as ONGC and InterGlobe Aviation behaved within different event windows. For ONGC, one short-window estimate yielded a p-value around .08: suggestive at a 10% threshold, but not conventionally significant at 5%. InterGlobe Aviation exhibited a short-term decline followed by recovery in some specifications. The details depend on the event definition and the chosen comparison period.

What I take from it

Research on political events should not collapse many dates and market mechanisms into a single dramatic claim. Even when a statistically detectable reaction appears, assigning that movement solely to one security event requires care about contemporaneous information and counterfactual trends.

The manuscript developed through the Young Researchers Initiative with guidance including Dr Anamika Singh and Vishal Kumar. It has been submitted and presented to academic venues, with conference acceptance distinct from journal publication.

NSRI / Finelytics

From state primacy to startup facilitation.

Seven decades of policy change are harder to explain than one neat “before and after.”

From State Primacy to Startup Facilitation: India's Policy Evolution (1956–2025) examines changes in how India's state, legal framework and economic institutions have approached private enterprise.

What the historical arc reveals

We traced a policy transition across state-led industrialisation, licensing, liberalisation, new entrepreneurial institutions and modern startup-support initiatives. The interesting question was not whether India simply became “more pro-business”; it was how administrative rules, industrial priorities, legal changes and economic conditions shaped the opportunity to start and sustain an enterprise.

What makes a policy-history paper difficult

Documents don't speak for themselves. Different laws take effect at different times, and changes in entrepreneurial outcomes may also reflect technology, financing conditions or macroeconomic shocks. Feedback on our draft made this clear: we needed to sharpen the coding rubric, specify inclusion rules and distinguish 2016–2024 outcomes from legal changes introduced in 2025. The paper's documentary findings should not be described as causal estimates of a policy's economic effects.

Vocational education / finance / formal modelling

Can incentives change the path to completing a skill?

SKILLFLOW grew from an interest in how vocational training, financing structures and contractual milestones interact. What began as a broad financing idea evolved through multiple manuscript revisions into an increasingly formal model of incentives and endogenous completion milestones.

The underlying tension

Training requires time, effort and money. Learners, providers and funders may want different things and face different risks. A contract that pays for enrolment rather than genuine progress can reward the wrong behaviour; a contract with overly strict milestones can exclude people who most need support.

How the project changed

Early submissions prompted critical editorial feedback about theoretical novelty, empirical grounding and the fit between the question and journal audiences. Instead of treating each rejection as an administrative problem, I revised the model, explored sensitivity analyses, and worked toward reproducible code supporting the assumptions. The newer manuscript examines endogenous contract milestones rather than merely describing an education-finance intervention.

What that process taught me

Formal modelling can expose how assumptions shape a result, even before any real-world evaluation is possible. Reworking SKILLFLOW taught me to treat editorial criticism as an invitation to sharpen a question rather than simply rewrite the abstract.
