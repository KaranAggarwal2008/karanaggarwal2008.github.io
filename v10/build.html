Four days of financial literacy

From 11–14 August 2025, we ran Financial Literacy Week across our school. Roughly 1,050–1,100 answer sheets came back from quiz rounds reaching over a thousand students in nearly 50 classrooms. Thirty-two students advanced to the finals. Behind the numbers were the less visible parts: preparing sheets, handling rooms, coordinating volunteers, interpreting results and answering last-minute logistical questions.

Our event team extended well beyond the company officers.

Pulkit Malik
Pulkit Malik

Sub-head of auditorium and logistics during Financial Literacy Week, with responsibilities involving junior-class quizzes. Also involved in the school Cubing Club.

contributed to auditorium and logistics and the junior-class quizzes. Many other student volunteers made the activities work; it would be misleading to reduce an operation of this size to four names.

Why the outcome mattered

The project reported about ₹60,000 in profit and a dividend worth 2.5 times the original share price. That was a business result; the deeper result was seeing financial language become accessible to younger students through a student-run event. We learned what people responded to, where communication broke down, and how important it was to keep a large team moving.

It was one thing to make a spreadsheet work. It was another to make the day work.

And on a school event day, everything seems to happen at once.

Student leadership

Helping someone else find a first opportunity.

As President of the school Internship Committee, I worked with a team of roughly 50 students to help others find and pursue internship opportunities.

More than 50 students were placed. Much of the work happened through one-to-one mentoring: listening to what somebody wanted, helping them communicate it, and encouraging them through applications they might otherwise have abandoned. Two placements involved IIM Indore internships.

I came to think of the committee as a small bridge between ambition and access. The person receiving the opportunity mattered much more than the name of the committee.

IIM Bangalore BBA DBE / Blueprint Bharat

Sometimes the problem isn't the product. It's the first five minutes.

Our Skillzo case focused on a nonprofit venture facing an onboarding problem: students were joining through Tally forms and WhatsApp groups without always understanding what Skillzo actually did or why a particular opportunity had reached them.

The challenge wasn't merely to make a prettier landing page. We explored whether a registration form was necessary, what information it should collect, how students should learn the value proposition, and whether directly joining a community might create a more welcoming beginning.

We also had to confront a familiar constraint: the organisation's team had limited bandwidth, and several of the potential fixes were already somewhere in its pipeline. Proposing more features without accounting for the ability to execute them would have missed the point.
