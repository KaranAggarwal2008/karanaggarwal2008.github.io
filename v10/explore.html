A little more about me.

When I finished school, I thought I would finally have some time to relax. That did not really happen.

Before I was thinking about economics papers or running a student company, I was learning to make small things with code, experimenting with quizzes and discovering that being curious could be a way of finding people. I was quieter when I was younger. Participating in quizzes gradually helped me find my voice.

I'm interested in business, public policy, international relations, law and psychology, but I don't experience them as neat, separate disciplines. Most of the questions I enjoy sit somewhere between them.

I like writing—including poetry—playing music, travelling, meeting people from different backgrounds and occasionally going down a rabbit hole about an idea no one asked me to investigate. It isn't always a balanced schedule, but it is a fairly accurate picture.

There is more of my everyday life on Instagram. I keep the original work here so this website remains useful even without a social-media account.

Hanoi, Vietnam / August 2026

HPAIR and the power of being in the room.

Conference rooms, policy simulations, teammates and a city I was discovering at the same time.

I had participated in HPAIR's February 2026 virtual conference before arriving at the Asia Conference in Hanoi. The virtual format introduced me to interdisciplinary teams; Hanoi made the experience far more immediate.

From a virtual challenge to Hanoi

At the virtual conference, our NFTE AI Impact Challenge team reached the top four in its track and top eight among 37 participating teams. In Hanoi, I took part in the Dynamic Policy Simulation and received the Best Speaker Award. The simulation required making arguments with incomplete information, balancing different interests and responding as other participants shifted the discussion.

Representing China's position in the simulation

In the policy simulation, I had to work with the China delegation's interests on Southeast Asian technology and security questions. Siya Punyani was our lead delegate. I argued for institutional checks rather than leaving rule-setting entirely to either the United States or China, supported ASEAN-oriented proposals with conditions and amendments, and resisted the idea that China would simply disclose sensitive technology. It was a good reminder that diplomacy involves understanding a position, not performing an uncomplicated version of your own.

The first time abroad on my own

Hanoi was also my first trip outside India alone. Between the conference venues—Almaz Convention Center, VinUniversity and the Diplomatic Academy of Vietnam—I had to manage unfamiliar routes, expenses, accents and unexpected decisions. I remember moments when I genuinely wasn't sure which direction I was going. Figuring it out mattered to me more than I expected: independence felt different once I'd actually had to practise it.

The impact challenge beyond the award

I was also involved in the VietHope Impact Challenge, a separate collaborative project. That team included Achira Mishra, Deepak Chhetry, Dhanya Venkateswaran, Douglas Anderson, Minh Tuan Le and Samvida Singhal. It belongs to the group experience rather than being folded into my individual speaker award.

The best memories aren't solely from the sessions. They include preparing with people I'd only recently met, navigating an unfamiliar place, and realising that the conversation sometimes continued long after the official programme ended.

IIM Ranchi / Young Changemakers Programme

The map wasn't the territory.

From applying and being assessed to meeting a real community problem.

YCP has been part of my journey more than once. In an earlier edition, I made it through the statement-of-purpose and quiz stages but didn't clear the final interview. I returned for YCP 4.0 and was selected among roughly 100 participants from several thousand applicants.

During the five-day bootcamp at IIM Ranchi, our team travelled to Lohajimi village in the Torpa–Khunti area. We studied the impact of elephant movement on crops, household income and safety. Suddenly the word “solution” had a much heavier meaning. What sounded compelling in a classroom had to account for local realities, livelihoods and the people who would live with its consequences.

A community that crosses borders

Teaching is another way of learning.

At Schoolhouse.world, I've hosted more than 60 tutoring sessions across mathematics, SAT preparation and statistics, and served in dialogue and editorial leadership roles.

As Dialogue President, I've seen how easily a thoughtful participant can be left out—not for lack of an idea, but because of language, confidence or a technical difficulty. When needed, I've entered rooms to speak more slowly or use translation tools so everyone could take part.

The experience reshaped how I think about leadership: sometimes the most useful thing you do is create the conditions for somebody else to speak.
